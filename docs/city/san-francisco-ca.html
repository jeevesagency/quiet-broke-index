San Francisco Quiet-Broke Index: How squeezed is a $400K household in San Francisco?

San Francisco earns a Quiet-Broke score of 60 out of 100, ranking it 2 of 30 metros tracked in this index — the higher the score, the more squeezed a $400K household feels. San Francisco's pain isn't taxes — the effective rate at $400K (39.8%) is within a percentage point of the 30-metro median. It's housing. The median 3BR is $4,500/month, or 13.5% of gross before tax — meaningfully above the 8.3% median. After the five fixed costs and taxes, the slack on a $400K HHI here is about $108,920 a year — real money, but a long way from "rich." For the full methodology behind these numbers, see the methodology page and the original Henry Finance deep dive on the Quiet-Broke Index.

What drives San Francisco's score

Five line items go into the index. Here is how San Francisco stacks up on each, relative to the 30-metro median:

Housing

Housing is the dominant cost — $4,500/month for a median 3BR is 1.64x the 30-metro median of $2,750. That single line item eats 13.5% of gross income before you've paid a dollar in tax, versus a 8.3% median across all 30 metros.

Taxes

Taxes are near the 30-metro median. The combined effective rate for MFJ at $400K is 39.8% (median: 38.3%), which translates to $159,200 in annual taxes. No particular advantage or disadvantage here.

Childcare

Childcare costs sting. Full-time daycare runs $2,640/month per child — for two kids, that's $63,360/year, or $25,440 more than the 30-metro median of $1,580/month. These are post-tax dollars, so the real gross equivalent at 40% effective rate is closer to $105,249.

Healthcare

Family health premiums (employee share) run $740/month — $50/month above the 30-metro median. At $8,880/year, this is a smaller line item than housing or taxes, but it's not nothing.

Transport

Transport comes in at $470/month all-in — close to the 30-metro median. At $5,640/year, it's a steady fixed cost but not what's moving the needle on San Francisco's score.

Compare to other metros

Context matters. Here are the cities that score closest to San Francisco, and what the comparison reveals about different ways to arrive at the same squeeze level:

  • New York, NY (score 66) scores 6 points less squeezed than San Francisco. The difference in post-fixed-cost slack is $12,200/year — meaningful but not city-changing. The two metros make an instructive comparison for households deciding between them.
  • San Jose, CA (score 59, rank 3) is the closest comparable — nearly identical squeeze levels despite different cost structures. San Jose has housing at $4,350/month and taxes at 39.8%, while San Francisco runs $4,500/month and 39.8% respectively. Same total pain, different sources.
  • Seattle, WA (score 40) is notably more squeezed than San Francisco. The gap is mostly housing — San Francisco's $4,500 median rent versus $3,500. A $400K household in Seattle keeps $150,440/year post-fixed-costs versus $108,920 in San Francisco.

Who feels the squeeze most in San Francisco

Take a dual-income couple in San Francisco: both work in tech or finance, combined W-2 of $400K, two kids in full-time care, renting a median 3BR. Their tax bill is $159,200/year. Housing eats another $54,000/year. Two kids in daycare at $2,640/month each: $63,360/year. Health and transport add $14,520/year. Total fixed obligations: $291,080 — leaving $108,920 for everything else. That's retirement contributions, college savings, vacations, car repairs, and any savings above the 401(k). It's workable, but it's not the "you're rich" number most people assume $400K to be.

What the data leaves out

The tax rate used here is an effective rate estimate for a $400K MFJ household in San Francisco — it blends federal, state, and city income tax with payroll taxes, and does not account for RSU timing, alternative minimum tax, or SALT deduction phase-outs, all of which can shift your actual bill by several percentage points.

The childcare figure covers full-time daycare for young children. Once kids reach school age, the choice between public and private school in San Francisco can swing this line item by $15,000 to $45,000/year per child — a scenario the index doesn't model.

This model assumes a two-earner household. Single-income households at $400K face a structurally different tax profile (often more favorable at this income level due to bracket differences), and households where one partner has large RSU cliff vests will see income concentration risk that changes the picture significantly from year to year.
