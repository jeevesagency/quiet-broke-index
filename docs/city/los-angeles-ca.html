Los Angeles Quiet-Broke Index: How squeezed is a $400K household in Los Angeles?

Los Angeles earns a Quiet-Broke score of 54 out of 100, ranking it 5 of 30 metros tracked in this index — the higher the score, the more squeezed a $400K household feels. Los Angeles's pain isn't taxes — the effective rate at $400K (39.8%) is within a percentage point of the 30-metro median. It's housing. The median 3BR is $4,100/month, or 12.3% of gross before tax — meaningfully above the 8.3% median. After the five fixed costs and taxes, the slack on a $400K HHI here is about $129,200 a year — real money, but a long way from "rich." For the full methodology behind these numbers, see the methodology page and the original Henry Finance deep dive on the Quiet-Broke Index.

What drives Los Angeles's score

Five line items go into the index. Here is how Los Angeles stacks up on each, relative to the 30-metro median:

Housing

Housing runs $4,100/month for a median 3BR — well above the 30-metro median of $2,750 (8.3% of gross). At 12.3% of a $400K gross, it's the largest single drain in the budget.

Taxes

Taxes are near the 30-metro median. The combined effective rate for MFJ at $400K is 39.8% (median: 38.3%), which translates to $159,200 in annual taxes. No particular advantage or disadvantage here.

Childcare

Childcare costs sting. Full-time daycare runs $1,980/month per child — for two kids, that's $47,520/year, or $9,600 more than the 30-metro median of $1,580/month. These are post-tax dollars, so the real gross equivalent at 40% effective rate is closer to $78,937.

Healthcare

Family health premiums (employee share) run $730/month — $40/month above the 30-metro median. At $8,760/year, this is a smaller line item than housing or taxes, but it's not nothing.

Transport

Transport runs $510/month all-in (two cars plus insurance and fuel, or a hybrid transit/rideshare mix), which is above average for the index. At $6,120/year, it adds meaningful pressure given the rest of the cost stack.

Compare to other metros

Context matters. Here are the cities that score closest to Los Angeles, and what the comparison reveals about different ways to arrive at the same squeeze level:

  • San Francisco, CA (score 60) scores 6 points less squeezed than Los Angeles. The difference in post-fixed-cost slack is $20,280/year — meaningful but not city-changing. The two metros make an instructive comparison for households deciding between them.
  • San Jose, CA (score 59, rank 3) is the closest comparable — nearly identical squeeze levels despite different cost structures. San Jose has housing at $4,350/month and taxes at 39.8%, while Los Angeles runs $4,100/month and 39.8% respectively. Same total pain, different sources.
  • Atlanta, GA (score 32) is notably more squeezed than Los Angeles. The gap is mostly housing — Los Angeles's $4,100 median rent versus $2,350. A $400K household in Atlanta keeps $172,640/year post-fixed-costs versus $129,200 in Los Angeles.

Who feels the squeeze most in Los Angeles

Consider a dual-income Los Angeles household earning $400K combined: federal and state taxes claim $159,200/year. Rent at the median 3BR costs $49,200/year. Two kids in full-time care: $47,520/year. Health premiums and transport add $14,880/year. Fixed obligations total $270,800, leaving $129,200/year. Compare that to the same income in Nashville: the post-fixed slack there is $194,240. The $65,040 gap is what "location arbitrage" actually looks like in dollar terms.

What the data leaves out

The tax rate used here is an effective rate estimate for a $400K MFJ household in Los Angeles — it blends federal, state, and city income tax with payroll taxes, and does not account for RSU timing, alternative minimum tax, or SALT deduction phase-outs, all of which can shift your actual bill by several percentage points.

The childcare figure covers full-time daycare for young children. Once kids reach school age, the choice between public and private school in Los Angeles can swing this line item by $15,000 to $45,000/year per child — a scenario the index doesn't model.

This model assumes a two-earner household. Single-income households at $400K face a structurally different tax profile (often more favorable at this income level due to bracket differences), and households where one partner has large RSU cliff vests will see income concentration risk that changes the picture significantly from year to year.
