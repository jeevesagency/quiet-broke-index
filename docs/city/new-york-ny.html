New York Quiet-Broke Index: How squeezed is a $400K household in New York?

New York earns a Quiet-Broke score of 66 out of 100, ranking it 1 of 30 metros tracked in this index — the higher the score, the more squeezed a $400K household feels. New York's squeeze isn't one thing — it's two. The median 3BR runs $4,900/month (14.7% of a $400K gross, vs. 8.3% for the 30-metro median), and the combined effective tax rate at $400K MFJ is 42.1% — 3.8 points above the median metro. After the five fixed costs and taxes, the slack on a $400K HHI here is about $96,720 a year — real money, but a long way from "rich." For the full methodology behind these numbers, see the methodology page and the original Henry Finance deep dive on the Quiet-Broke Index.

What drives New York's score

Five line items go into the index. Here is how New York stacks up on each, relative to the 30-metro median:

Housing

Housing is the dominant cost — $4,900/month for a median 3BR is 1.78x the 30-metro median of $2,750. That single line item eats 14.7% of gross income before you've paid a dollar in tax, versus a 8.3% median across all 30 metros.

Taxes

Taxes are a meaningful drag. At 42.1% combined effective rate for MFJ at $400K, New York runs 3.8 points above the 30-metro median of 38.3%. That difference is roughly $15,200 more in annual taxes than the median metro — money that simply doesn't land in your account.

Childcare

Childcare costs sting. Full-time daycare runs $2,580/month per child — for two kids, that's $61,920/year, or $24,000 more than the 30-metro median of $1,580/month. These are post-tax dollars, so the real gross equivalent at 42% effective rate is closer to $106,943.

Healthcare

Family health premiums (employee share) run $760/month — $70/month above the 30-metro median. At $9,120/year, this is a smaller line item than housing or taxes, but it's not nothing.

Transport

Transport costs are lower than most metros at $420/month all-in. At $5,040/year, this is one area where New York provides a modest offset against higher costs elsewhere.

Compare to other metros

Context matters. Here are the cities that score closest to New York, and what the comparison reveals about different ways to arrive at the same squeeze level:

  • San Francisco, CA (score 60) scores 6 points more squeezed than New York. The difference in post-fixed-cost slack is $12,200/year — meaningful but not city-changing. The two metros make an instructive comparison for households deciding between them.
  • San Jose, CA (score 59) scores 7 points more squeezed than New York. The difference in post-fixed-cost slack is $19,640/year — meaningful but not city-changing. The two metros make an instructive comparison for households deciding between them.
  • Portland, OR (score 41) is notably more squeezed than New York. The gap is mostly housing — New York's $4,900 median rent versus $2,950. A $400K household in Portland keeps $147,480/year post-fixed-costs versus $96,720 in New York.

Who feels the squeeze most in New York

Take a dual-income couple in New York: both work in tech or finance, combined W-2 of $400K, two kids in full-time care, renting a median 3BR. Their tax bill is $168,400/year. Housing eats another $58,800/year. Two kids in daycare at $2,580/month each: $61,920/year. Health and transport add $14,160/year. Total fixed obligations: $303,280 — leaving $96,720 for everything else. That's retirement contributions, college savings, vacations, car repairs, and any savings above the 401(k). It's workable, but it's not the "you're rich" number most people assume $400K to be.

What the data leaves out

The tax rate used here is an effective rate estimate for a $400K MFJ household in New York — it blends federal, state, and city income tax with payroll taxes, and does not account for RSU timing, alternative minimum tax, or SALT deduction phase-outs, all of which can shift your actual bill by several percentage points.

The childcare figure covers full-time daycare for young children. Once kids reach school age, the choice between public and private school in New York can swing this line item by $15,000 to $45,000/year per child — a scenario the index doesn't model.

This model assumes a two-earner household. Single-income households at $400K face a structurally different tax profile (often more favorable at this income level due to bracket differences), and households where one partner has large RSU cliff vests will see income concentration risk that changes the picture significantly from year to year.
