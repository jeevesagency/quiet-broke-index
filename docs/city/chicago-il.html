Chicago Quiet-Broke Index: How squeezed is a $400K household in Chicago?

Chicago earns a Quiet-Broke score of 38 out of 100, ranking it 14 of 30 metros tracked in this index — the higher the score, the more squeezed a $400K household feels. Chicago ranks 14 of 30 not because any single line item is brutal, but because all five are above-average at once. Housing is $2,950/month, childcare runs $1,840/kid, and the combined effective tax rate is 38.8%. None of these alone would make the news. Stacked, they consume 62.0% of a $400K gross. After the five fixed costs and taxes, the slack on a $400K HHI here is about $151,920 a year — real money, but a long way from "rich." For the full methodology behind these numbers, see the methodology page and the original Henry Finance deep dive on the Quiet-Broke Index.

What drives Chicago's score

Five line items go into the index. Here is how Chicago stacks up on each, relative to the 30-metro median:

Housing

Housing is close to the 30-metro median, at $2,950/month (8.8% of gross) versus a metro-wide median of $2,750 (8.3% of gross). Not cheap, not the headline story.

Taxes

Taxes are near the 30-metro median. The combined effective rate for MFJ at $400K is 38.8% (median: 38.3%), which translates to $155,200 in annual taxes. No particular advantage or disadvantage here.

Childcare

Childcare is roughly average. Full-time care runs $1,840/month per child — for two kids, $44,160/year total. The 30-metro median is $1,580/month, so Chicago is within $260/month of dead center.

Healthcare

Health premiums (employee share) are close to the 30-metro average at $700/month, or $8,400/year. Not a distinguishing factor in Chicago's score.

Transport

Transport costs are lower than most metros at $410/month all-in. At $4,920/year, this is one area where Chicago provides a modest offset against higher costs elsewhere.

Compare to other metros

Context matters. Here are the cities that score closest to Chicago, and what the comparison reveals about different ways to arrive at the same squeeze level:

  • Portland, OR (score 41, rank 9) is the closest comparable — nearly identical squeeze levels despite different cost structures. Portland has housing at $2,950/month and taxes at 41.2%, while Chicago runs $2,950/month and 38.8% respectively. Same total pain, different sources.
  • Seattle, WA (score 40, rank 10) is the closest comparable — nearly identical squeeze levels despite different cost structures. Seattle has housing at $3,500/month and taxes at 34.1%, while Chicago runs $2,950/month and 38.8% respectively. Same total pain, different sources.
  • New York, NY (score 66) scores 28 points less squeezed than Chicago. The difference in post-fixed-cost slack is $55,200/year — meaningful but not city-changing. The two metros make an instructive comparison for households deciding between them.

Who feels the squeeze most in Chicago

Consider a dual-income Chicago household earning $400K combined: federal and state taxes claim $155,200/year. Rent at the median 3BR costs $35,400/year. Two kids in full-time care: $44,160/year. Health premiums and transport add $13,320/year. Fixed obligations total $248,080, leaving $151,920/year. Compare that to the same income in New York: the post-fixed slack there is $96,720. The $55,200 gap is what "location arbitrage" actually looks like in dollar terms.

What the data leaves out

The tax rate used here is an effective rate estimate for a $400K MFJ household in Chicago — it blends federal, state, and city income tax with payroll taxes, and does not account for RSU timing, alternative minimum tax, or SALT deduction phase-outs, all of which can shift your actual bill by several percentage points.

The childcare figure covers full-time daycare for young children. Once kids reach school age, the choice between public and private school in Chicago can swing this line item by $15,000 to $45,000/year per child — a scenario the index doesn't model.

This model assumes a two-earner household. Single-income households at $400K face a structurally different tax profile (often more favorable at this income level due to bracket differences), and households where one partner has large RSU cliff vests will see income concentration risk that changes the picture significantly from year to year.
